Understanding Your Taxable Wages in Illinois

Your taxable wages are the portion of your paycheck subject to tax. This is usually your gross pay minus certain pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions or health insurance premiums. Federal taxable wages and Illinois state taxable wages can differ slightly depending on which deductions the state allows.

For most workers, taxable wages are close to gross pay minus any pre-tax benefits you elect. Our calculator applies the correct taxable wage base automatically based on your entries.

Local Income Taxes in Illinois

Illinois has no separate local income tax in most areas, though some municipalities charge small additional fees. Chicago itself does not add a local wage tax.

Form W-4 and Withholding in Illinois

When you start a new job, you complete a federal Form W-4. This form tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold taxes from each paycheck. Your filing status, dependents, and any additional withholding you request all affect this calculation.

Filling out your Form W-4 correctly matters. If you withhold too little, you may owe money at tax time. If you withhold too much, you get a bigger refund but less money in each paycheck throughout the year. Our calculator shows you the standard withholding amount based on current 2026 tax brackets.

Payroll Taxes: What Comes Out of Every Illinois Paycheck

Every Illinois paycheck has several types of payroll taxes withheld. Understanding each one helps you read your pay stub with confidence.

  • Federal income tax: Based on 2026 IRS tax brackets, from 10% to 37%
  • Illinois state tax: a flat 4.95% rate
  • FICA taxes: Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%)

FICA taxes fund Social Security and Medicare programs nationwide. Every worker pays these regardless of state. Your employer also pays a matching amount on your behalf, though this employer portion doesn't come out of your paycheck.

State Unemployment Tax — Does It Affect Your Paycheck?

State unemployment tax in Illinois is primarily paid by employers, not employees. This tax funds unemployment benefits for workers who lose their jobs. It typically does not appear as a deduction on your pay stub, since the employer covers the full cost in most states including Illinois.

Gross Pay vs Net Pay: What's the Difference?

Your gross pay is your full salary before any deductions. Your net pay — also called take-home pay — is what actually lands in your bank account after federal tax, Illinois state tax, and FICA taxes are withheld.
